On the Grand-Place, one facade stands out from all the others: no gable, but a balustrade topped by an octagonal lantern tower and a statue of Fame blowing a trumpet. This is Le Roy d'Espagne, the former bakers' guild house, built in 1697 just after the bombardment of Brussels. Today it is a brasserie open from morning to night, in a listed Baroque setting.

## What to do there

You can come at any time of day, which is what makes it so handy: breakfast in the morning, lunch at noon, an afternoon snack or a late dinner. The cooking is Belgian and billed as homemade, based on fresh, local produce. Among the classics the house highlights: homemade grey shrimp croquettes, Flemish carbonade slow-cooked with a hint of gingerbread and Liège syrup, mussels and fries served in a pot, and entrecôte with béarnaise sauce.

Before you sit down, look up. The facade reads like a book: above the door, the gilded bust of Saint Aubert, patron saint of bakers; on the second floor, the bust of Charles II, King of Spain, who gave the house its name; on the balustrade, six statues representing what it takes to make bread, from wheat to wind, by way of fire and water. The brasserie's website tells this story in detail.

The terrace, set right on the Grand-Place, looks straight onto the Town Hall. For groups, several private rooms can host up to 150 people.

## Who it's for

For anyone showing Brussels to friends or family who wants to eat in the heart of the Grand-Place, for history buffs, and for everyone looking for a table that is open early in the morning as well as late at night. The setting and the view alone are worth the trip, kids included.
