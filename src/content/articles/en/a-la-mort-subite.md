Opposite the entrance to the Galeries Royales Saint-Hubert, a stone's throw from the Grand-Place, À la Mort Subite has barely changed. Large arched mirrors, columns, benches and small wooden tables: the original interior is protected, and the house describes itself as one of the oldest cafés in the capital. Jacques Brel was a regular, as were many artists, writers and actors.

## A name born from a dice game

The name makes people curious, and the story the house tells begins around 1910. Théophile Vossen was then running La Cour Royale, on the corner of rue d'Assaut and rue de la Montagne. Many of his regulars worked with the National Bank and killed time playing pitjesbak, a dice game that later became 421. The loser was "the dead man". When time was short, they played one last round in a single throw: the "mort subite", or sudden death. The expression caught on so well among the regulars that the owner made it his sign. In late 1928 he moved, while staying in the neighbourhood. Today Bernard and Olivier Vossen, the fourth generation of the family, run the place.

## What to do there

You sit elbow to elbow, locals and visitors side by side, in the atmosphere of an old bistro. The food is a short menu: slices of bread with fromage blanc (the house speciality), filled sandwiches, omelettes, local cheeses and charcuterie, with vegetarian options. For drinks, the classics sit alongside a good choice of gueuzes and beers from Belgium and elsewhere.

## Who it's for

A break after a walk through the Galeries, visitors keen to see a Brussels café as it has always been, anyone who enjoys the city's history. The house's website also says the place suits families and is accessible to people with reduced mobility.
