You walk in, look up, and the name makes sense. La Quincaillerie was founded in 1903 as a hardware shop, in a building designed by students of Victor Horta, whose house (now the Horta Museum) is right next door. The shop stayed in the family until the late 1970s, then became a restaurant. In 1988, architect Antoine Pinto redesigned the interior while keeping everything that gave it character: the large entrance hall, the grand staircase topped by a giant clock, the drawers, the counters and the original tiled floor. A nice detail: the room behind the clock was once the manager's office.

## What to eat

Seasonal Belgian brasserie cooking, made with produce from local suppliers. The house is known for its seafood: oysters from several origins, including the Kara Savi, developed especially for the brasserie by oyster farmer Véronique Gillardeau, and platters to share, from the Plateau Quincaillerie to the Plateau Royal. From the kitchen, you'll find homemade shrimp croquettes, North Sea sole with fresh fries, moules marinière, Flemish carbonnade, a Bresse chicken vol-au-vent, a Black Angus steak or an artichoke risotto. The poultry and charcuterie come from Le Devant, a farm in Bresse owned by the brasserie. For dessert: Brussels waffle, dame blanche, crème brûlée or tarte tatin. A lunch menu changes every week.

## Who it's for

For a dinner with family or friends in a setting you won't forget, for introducing visitors to Belgian cooking after the Horta Museum, or for a group meal: the brasserie hosts from 10 to 200 people. The Châtelain neighbourhood, with its shops, is just around the corner.
